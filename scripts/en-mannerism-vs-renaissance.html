Put Raphael's School of Athens next to Parmigianino's Madonna with the Long Neck and the difference hits you at once. In Raphael, philosophers stand calmly in a vast, perfectly ordered hall. In Parmigianino, the Virgin's neck stretches like a swan's, the baby seems too large, and a row of columns trails off into nowhere. Only about twenty-five years separate the two paintings. That gap, and what happened in it, is the story of Renaissance versus Mannerism.

The short answer

The High Renaissance (roughly 1490 to 1527) aimed at balance, harmony, clear space and idealised but believable figures. Mannerism (roughly 1520 to 1600) took the skills of the Renaissance and bent them: elongated bodies, crowded or illogical space, unusual colours and deliberately complex poses. Renaissance art wants to look natural. Mannerist art wants to look artful.

  • Proportions: Renaissance figures follow classical ideals. Mannerist figures are stretched, twisted and elegant.
  • Space: Renaissance space is built with clear linear perspective. Mannerist space is compressed, ambiguous or unstable.
  • Composition: Renaissance pictures are centred and stable. Mannerist pictures push action to the edges or pile figures up.
  • Colour: Renaissance colour is natural and unified. Mannerist colour is often acidic, cool or surprising.
  • Mood: Renaissance art feels calm and confident. Mannerist art feels refined, restless or strange.

The High Renaissance: the ideal of balance

By around 1500, painters in Florence and Rome had solved most of the technical problems that had occupied artists for a century: linear perspective, anatomy, convincing light. Leonardo, Raphael and Michelangelo used those tools to create images of extraordinary clarity. In the School of Athens (1509-11, Vatican), Raphael arranges dozens of figures across a symmetrical architectural stage, with Plato and Aristotle exactly at the vanishing point. Everything is legible. Nothing is out of place.

What changed after 1520

Raphael died in 1520. In 1527 imperial troops sacked Rome, scattering artists and patrons. Religious conflict was spreading across Europe after the Reformation. Many historians link Mannerism to this sense of crisis, though others stress a simpler artistic motive: once the High Renaissance had perfected naturalism, younger painters had to find something new to do. The name comes from the Italian maniera, "style" or "manner", a word that the sixteenth-century writer Giorgio Vasari used as praise for grace and sophistication. Only later did "mannered" become an insult.

Mannerism in three paintings

Parmigianino, Madonna with the Long Neck (1534-40, Uffizi). The Virgin's neck, fingers and body are drawn out to an impossible length, and the Christ Child lies across her lap at an uneasy angle. On the right, a tiny prophet stands beside a column that seems to belong to a different scale altogether. The painting was left unfinished, which adds to its strangeness. We look at it in more detail in our article on the Madonna with the Long Neck.

Pontormo, Deposition from the Cross (1525-28, Santa Felicita, Florence). The title promises a cross, but there is none. Figures in pale pinks and blues float in a space with no ground and no horizon, holding the body of Christ as if weightless. The emotional effect is intense, but it comes from colour and pose, not from a believable scene.

Bronzino, Venus, Cupid, Folly and Time (c. 1545, National Gallery, London). Polished, cold and full of riddles, this allegory packs figures into a shallow strip of space. Smooth marble-like skin, masks and hidden symbols reward slow looking. It is Mannerism at its most courtly and intellectual.

Sculpture: from one view to every view

The difference shows in sculpture too. Michelangelo's Pietà (1498-99) is designed to be seen mainly from the front, a stable pyramid of grief. Giambologna's Abduction of a Sabine Woman (1579-83, Loggia dei Lanzi, Florence) twists three bodies into an upward spiral so that no single viewpoint shows the whole. This spiralling pose, called the figura serpentinata or "serpentine figure", is one of Mannerism's signature devices.

Was Michelangelo a Mannerist?

He is usually classed as a High Renaissance master, but his later work points the way. The muscular, twisting bodies of the Last Judgment (1536-41) and the strange architecture of the vestibule of the Laurentian Library in Florence, with columns sunk into the walls, broke the rules he had helped establish. Many Mannerists treated him as their model.

Is Mannerism a separate period?

Scholars still argue about this. Some see Mannerism as a distinct style between the Renaissance and the Baroque. Others treat it as a late phase of the Renaissance itself, since it uses the same training and techniques. For most practical purposes, the useful distinction is in the aims: the Renaissance tried to make art look like nature, and Mannerism tried to make art look like art.

How to tell them apart in a museum

Ask three questions. Could these bodies stand up and walk? If they seem too long or too twisted, think Mannerism. Can you measure the room with your eyes? If the space is clear and deep, think Renaissance. Does the colour look like daylight? If it is cool, shimmering or unexpected, Mannerism is likely.

FAQ

What is the main difference between Mannerism and the Renaissance?

The High Renaissance sought balance, natural proportions and clear space, while Mannerism deliberately distorted proportions, compressed space and used unusual colour to create elegance and tension.

When did Mannerism start?

Around 1520, after the death of Raphael, and it lasted until roughly 1600, when the Baroque took over.

Who are the main Mannerist artists?

Pontormo, Parmigianino, Rosso Fiorentino, Bronzino and Giambologna in Italy, and El Greco, whose late work carried Mannerist ideas into Spain.

Why is it called Mannerism?

From the Italian maniera, meaning style or manner, which Vasari used to describe the graceful, sophisticated style of artists working after Raphael and Michelangelo.
