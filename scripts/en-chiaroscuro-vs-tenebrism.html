Stand in front of a Leonardo and then a Caravaggio and you will feel the difference before you can name it. Both painters used strong contrasts of light and dark. In Leonardo the shadows melt softly into the figures. In Caravaggio, darkness swallows half the room and a single beam of light picks out a face, a hand, a coin. The first is chiaroscuro. The second is tenebrism. One grew out of the other, which is why the two words are so often confused.

The short answer

Chiaroscuro is the general technique of using graded light and shadow to make figures look solid and three-dimensional. Tenebrism is an extreme, theatrical form of chiaroscuro in which most of the picture sinks into deep darkness and the subject is lit as if by a spotlight. All tenebrism is chiaroscuro. Most chiaroscuro is not tenebrism.

  • Chiaroscuro: smooth transitions, plenty of mid-tones, a readable background. Goal: volume and realism.
  • Tenebrism: abrupt jumps from light to near-black, few mid-tones, background often lost in darkness. Goal: drama and focus.
  • Quick test: Can you see the room the figures are in? If yes, it is probably chiaroscuro. If the figures seem to step out of a black void, it is tenebrism.

Where the words come from

Chiaroscuro is Italian, from chiaro (light, clear) and scuro (dark). Renaissance writers used it for the modelling of form through light and shade, and it also names a type of sixteenth-century woodcut printed from several blocks in different tones. Tenebrism comes from the Italian tenebroso, "dark" or "murky". Painters working in this manner are sometimes called the tenebrosi. The word is mostly a later label applied by critics and historians, and some scholars think it is too loose to be a true category. It remains useful as a description.

Chiaroscuro: light that builds form

Renaissance painters developed chiaroscuro to solve a basic problem: how to make a flat surface look round. Light falling on a curved object produces a gradual change of tone, from highlight through half-tone and core shadow to reflected light. Reproduce that sequence and a painted face or a fold of cloth seems to turn in space.

Leonardo da Vinci pushed the technique further than anyone of his generation. In the Virgin of the Rocks (1483-86, Louvre), the figures emerge from a dim grotto, but their faces and hands are modelled with infinitely soft transitions. This softness is related to his sfumato, the smoky blending of tones without visible outlines. The shadows are deep, yet you can still read the rocks, the plants and the distant landscape.

Tenebrism: light as drama

Around 1600 Caravaggio changed the role of light. In The Calling of Saint Matthew (1599-1600, Contarelli Chapel, San Luigi dei Francesi, Rome), tax collectors sit counting money in a dark room. Christ enters on the right, almost hidden, and points. A shaft of light cuts diagonally across the wall and lands on Matthew's face. The light does not just describe forms. It tells the story, carrying the call from Christ to the man who is about to follow him.

The effect was so powerful that it spread across Europe within a generation. Followers known as the Caravaggisti took it to Naples, Utrecht, Spain and France. Artemisia Gentileschi used it to give her Judith Slaying Holofernes (c. 1620, Uffizi) its brutal clarity. Jusepe de Ribera and Francisco de Zurbarán made it central to Spanish religious painting. In Utrecht, Gerrit van Honthorst became so associated with night scenes that Italians nicknamed him Gherardo delle Notti, "Gerard of the Nights".

Candlelight: a special case

Some of the most beautiful tenebrist paintings use a visible light source inside the picture. Georges de La Tour's Magdalen with the Smoking Flame (c. 1640, Los Angeles County Museum of Art) shows Mary Magdalene in near-total darkness, her hand resting on a skull, lit only by a single candle. The flame itself becomes the subject of meditation. This is where tenebrism meets the vanitas tradition: the candle is also a symbol of a life burning down. Key differences side by side

  • Background: chiaroscuro keeps the setting visible; tenebrism lets it fall into darkness.
  • Transitions: chiaroscuro moves gradually through mid-tones; tenebrism jumps from bright light to deep shadow.
  • Light source: chiaroscuro often uses diffuse light; tenebrism favours a single, directional, often unseen source.
  • Purpose: chiaroscuro aims at volume and naturalism; tenebrism aims at focus, emotion and narrative.
  • Period: chiaroscuro runs through Western painting from the Renaissance onward; tenebrism is concentrated in the decades after 1600.
  • Typical artists: Leonardo, Raphael, Rembrandt for chiaroscuro; Caravaggio, Artemisia Gentileschi, Ribera, Zurbarán, La Tour for tenebrism.

Is Rembrandt chiaroscuro or tenebrism?

This is the most common borderline case. Rembrandt's shadows are deep and his light often falls like a spotlight, as in The Night Watch (1642, Rijksmuseum). But look into his darks and you will find texture, warm brown glazes and half-hidden figures. They are full rooms, not empty voids. Most art historians therefore describe Rembrandt as a supreme master of chiaroscuro who absorbed lessons from the Caravaggisti without becoming a strict tenebrist.

After the Baroque

Dramatic lighting never really disappeared. In eighteenth-century England, Joseph Wright of Derby painted scientific demonstrations by candlelight. In An Experiment on a Bird in the Air Pump (1768, National Gallery, London), a single hidden flame lights a circle of faces reacting to a bird struggling in a glass vessel. It is a Baroque device used for a modern subject. Film directors and photographers still talk about "Caravaggio lighting" when a scene uses one hard light against a dark background.

How to use both in your own work

If you draw or paint, the difference is easy to try. For chiaroscuro, light a simple object, an egg or a mug, with a lamp placed high and to one side, and study the full range of tones from highlight to reflected light. For tenebrism, switch off every other light, move the lamp closer and block the spill so that the background goes black. Then decide what you want the viewer to see first, and let only that catch the light.

FAQ

What is the difference between chiaroscuro and tenebrism?

Chiaroscuro is the use of graded light and shadow to model form. Tenebrism is a more extreme version in which deep darkness dominates and a strong, focused light isolates the main figures.

Is tenebrism a type of chiaroscuro?

Yes. Tenebrism is usually described as an intensified form of chiaroscuro, so every tenebrist painting uses chiaroscuro, but not the reverse.

Who invented tenebrism?

Caravaggio is credited with making it a defining style around 1600, though earlier painters had experimented with night scenes and strong contrasts.

Can one painting use both?

Yes. Many Baroque paintings model figures with soft chiaroscuro inside a tenebrist setting, so the faces look round and natural while the background stays dark.
