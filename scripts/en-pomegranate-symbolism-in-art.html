Few fruits have carried as much meaning as the pomegranate. Split it open and you find hundreds of seeds packed in red pulp, sealed inside a tough skin crowned with a little six-pointed calyx. Painters, weavers and poets have read that structure in very different ways: as death and return, as fertility, as the unity of the Church, as royal power. If a pomegranate appears in a painting, it is almost never there just because it looked nice.

Quick guide: what a pomegranate can mean

  • Death and rebirth: from the Greek myth of Persephone, who ate pomegranate seeds in the underworld.
  • Fertility and marriage: because of its many seeds, especially as an attribute of Venus and in wedding imagery.
  • Resurrection and eternal life: in Christian art, often in the hands of the Christ Child.
  • The Church: many seeds united inside one skin, an image of many believers in one faith.
  • Abundance and good fortune: in Ottoman, Persian and Chinese decorative arts.
  • Royalty: the crown-shaped calyx, and the badge of Granada and of Catherine of Aragon.

Persephone and the seeds of the underworld

The oldest and most dramatic meaning comes from Greek myth. When Hades carried Persephone to the underworld, she ate pomegranate seeds there, and that small act bound her to return to him for part of every year. Her descent became winter and her return became spring. In art, a pomegranate in a goddess's hand therefore signals a double life, death and renewal held in the same fruit. Dante Gabriel Rossetti's Proserpine (1874, Tate Britain) shows her holding the bitten fruit in a dim corridor. We look at that image and many others in our guide to Persephone in art.

Fertility, love and marriage

The sheer number of seeds made the pomegranate a natural symbol of fertility across the ancient Mediterranean. It was associated with Aphrodite, the Roman Venus, and appears in Renaissance paintings as one of her attributes, alongside roses and doves. In wedding portraits and marriage chests, a pomegranate could express the hope of many children. The same idea travelled east: in Chinese tradition the fruit became a wish for numerous sons, and it still appears in wedding decorations.

The pomegranate in Christian art

Christian painters inherited the fruit and gave it new meanings. Its blood-red juice suggested the Passion, and its return-from-death associations made it a symbol of resurrection. In Sandro Botticelli's Madonna of the Pomegranate (c. 1487, Uffizi), the Virgin holds an open fruit while the Christ Child rests his hand on it, a quiet reference to the suffering and the rising to come. Botticelli used the same motif in his earlier Madonna of the Magnificat, also in the Uffizi.

Theologians also read the many seeds inside a single skin as an image of the Church: countless members held together in one body. A pomegranate in a saint's or bishop's hand can carry that meaning. The fruit has deep roots in the Hebrew Bible as well. Exodus describes pomegranates embroidered along the hem of the high priest's robe, alternating with golden bells, and the fruit appears among the produce of the Promised Land and in the love poetry of the Song of Songs.

Royal pomegranates: Granada and Catherine of Aragon

The Spanish word for pomegranate is granada, and the fruit is the emblem of the city of Granada, the last Muslim kingdom in Spain, taken by Ferdinand and Isabella in 1492. Their daughter, Catherine of Aragon, carried the pomegranate badge with her to England when she married into the Tudor dynasty, and it appears in heraldry and decoration associated with her. Here the fruit is political: a statement of Spanish royal identity. Its crown-like calyx is often said to have inspired the shape of royal crowns, a pleasing idea, though it is more legend than documented history.

Pomegranates in Ottoman and Islamic art

The pomegranate was a favourite motif in Ottoman textiles, carpets and İznik tiles, usually as a stylised form surrounded by tulips, carnations and leaves. In these designs it stands for abundance, fertility and paradise. Islamic tradition counts the pomegranate among the fruits of paradise, and the Quran mentions it among God's gifts. Walk through the Topkapı Palace or look closely at a sixteenth-century Ottoman velvet and you will see the split fruit with its rows of seeds again and again. In Turkey it is still a popular good-luck symbol, broken on the doorstep at New Year so that the coming year will be as full as the fruit.

Pomegranates in still life

In seventeenth-century Dutch and Spanish still lifes, a pomegranate split open beside other fruit can simply be a display of skill, since its glossy seeds were a challenge to paint. But it often adds a quiet note of meaning. A cracked or overripe pomegranate in a vanitas still life speaks of beauty that will spoil. How to read a pomegranate in a painting

Start with who is holding it. A goddess in a dark setting points to Persephone and the cycle of death and return. Venus or a bride points to love and fertility. The Christ Child or the Virgin points to Passion and resurrection. A queen's portrait or a coat of arms points to Granada and Spanish royalty. Then check whether the fruit is whole or split. An open pomegranate, seeds on show, almost always invites you to think about what is inside: life, many lives, or a life that will return.

FAQ

What does a pomegranate symbolize in art?

Depending on context, it can mean death and rebirth (Persephone), fertility and marriage (Venus), resurrection and the Church (Christian art), abundance and paradise (Islamic and Ottoman art) or royal identity (Granada and Catherine of Aragon).

Why does the Christ Child hold a pomegranate?

In paintings like Botticelli's Madonna of the Pomegranate, the red fruit foreshadows Christ's Passion, and its link with returning from death makes it a symbol of the Resurrection.

What does the pomegranate mean in the Persephone myth?

Eating its seeds in the underworld bound Persephone to return there every year, so the fruit stands for the cycle of the seasons and for death followed by renewal.

Why is the pomegranate a symbol of Granada?

Because the Spanish word for pomegranate is granada. The fruit appears on the city's coat of arms and, through Ferdinand and Isabella, in the Spanish royal arms.
