A skull on a table does not automatically make a painting a vanitas. That small distinction trips up students, museum visitors and quite a few websites. Memento mori and vanitas share the same props, the skull, the hourglass, the guttering candle, but they are not the same thing. One is a very old idea that turns up almost everywhere. The other is a specific kind of picture that flourished in the Dutch Republic in the seventeenth century.

The short answer

Memento mori is the broad theme: any image or object meant to remind you that you will die. Vanitas is a narrower genre, usually a still life, that adds a second message: the things people chase in life, money, beauty, knowledge, pleasure, are empty because death will take them away. Every vanitas contains a memento mori. Not every memento mori is a vanitas.

  • Memento mori: "Remember that you must die." Found in portraits, tombs, jewellery, sculpture, prints and manuscripts from antiquity to today.
  • Vanitas: "Vanity." A type of still life, most popular in the Netherlands roughly between 1620 and 1670, that sets symbols of wealth and learning beside symbols of decay.
  • The test: If the picture only says "you will die", it is memento mori. If it also says "so your possessions and ambitions are worthless", you are looking at a vanitas.

Where the words come from

Memento mori is Latin and means "remember that you will die". The phrase became a fixture of Christian devotional culture in the Middle Ages, when the point of thinking about death was to live well enough to be ready for it. A popular story says a servant whispered the words to Roman generals during their triumphal parades. It makes a good anecdote, but the ancient sources for it are thin, so treat it as legend.

Vanitas comes from the opening of the Book of Ecclesiastes in the Latin Vulgate Bible: vanitas vanitatum, omnia vanitas, "vanity of vanities, all is vanity". Here "vanity" does not mean admiring yourself in the mirror. It means emptiness, something as insubstantial as breath. A vanitas painting is a sermon on that verse, delivered with silverware and tulips.

What is memento mori in art?

Because it is a theme rather than a genre, memento mori can appear inside almost any kind of image. In Hans Holbein the Younger's The Ambassadors (1533, National Gallery, London), two confident young diplomats stand beside a shelf of globes and scientific instruments. Across the floor runs a strange grey smear. Seen from the right-hand side of the painting, it resolves into a skull. Holbein did not paint a vanitas still life. He painted a double portrait with a memento mori hidden in plain sight.

The same logic applies to Frans Hals's Young Man with a Skull (c. 1626-28, National Gallery, London). The sitter is lively, almost cheerful, and the skull in his hand is the reminder. Outside painting, memento mori shows up in carved tomb effigies, in the "dance of death" prints of the late Middle Ages, and in mourning rings and pendants shaped like tiny coffins.

What is a vanitas painting?

Vanitas still lifes grew popular in the Dutch Republic in the early seventeenth century, with the university town of Leiden as an early centre. The formula is easy to recognise once you know it. A table is laid with objects that stand for the good things of life: books and globes for learning, coins, pearls and silver for wealth, lutes and violins for pleasure, flowers for beauty. Among them sit the signs of death and time.

Pieter Claesz's vanitas from around 1628 is a good example, and it contains a small joke. In the polished glass ball on the table you can make out a tiny reflection of the painter at his easel, as if to say that the artist, too, belongs among the things that will pass.

Harmen Steenwijck, who trained in Leiden, painted some of the most austere versions: a skull, a few books, a lamp that has just gone out, all in a narrow range of browns. The restraint is part of the message. There is nothing here worth wanting.

The symbols, and which side they belong to

Most symbols are shared. What changes is the balance. A memento mori needs only one sign of death. A vanitas needs the contrast between luxury and decay.

  • Skull: the core sign of death. Common to both.
  • Hourglass, watch or clock: time running out. Common to both.
  • Candle, snuffed or smoking: a life put out. Common to both.
  • Soap bubbles: life as fragile and brief as a bubble, from the Latin saying homo bulla, "man is a bubble". Mostly vanitas.
  • Wilting flowers, overripe fruit, flies: beauty that spoils. Mostly vanitas.
  • Books, globes, maps, instruments: the limits of human knowledge. Vanitas.
  • Coins, jewels, silver, shells: the vanity of wealth and collecting. Vanitas.
  • Musical instruments: pleasure that lasts only as long as the sound. Vanitas.
  • Laurel or crown: worldly fame. Vanitas.
  • Ears of wheat or ivy: sometimes added as a sign of resurrection and hope. Both.

Can one painting be both?

Yes, and many are. Philippe de Champaigne's Still Life with a Skull (c. 1671, Musée de Tessé, Le Mans) reduces the vanitas formula to three objects: a tulip, a skull and an hourglass. Read from left to right they become life, death and time. It is a vanitas, because the tulip was a famously costly luxury flower, and it is also about as pure a memento mori as painting ever produced.

The overlap explains why the terms get used interchangeably. A useful rule is to call the subject memento mori and the genre vanitas. You can say a vanitas still life carries a memento mori message, but not the other way round.

Why the Dutch painted so many

Three things came together in the seventeenth-century Netherlands. Calvinist preaching warned constantly against worldly attachment. At the same time, the Dutch Republic was one of the richest societies in Europe, full of merchants who bought paintings for their homes. And still life had become a respected specialty, with painters able to render silver, glass and petals with startling realism. The irony was not lost on anyone: a vanitas condemned luxury while being an expensive luxury object itself. Owning one let a wealthy household show both its taste and its piety.

Vanitas and memento mori today

Both ideas are alive. Contemporary artists keep returning to the skull, most famously Damien Hirst with For the Love of God (2007), a platinum cast of a human skull covered in diamonds, which turns the vanitas warning against wealth into its own punchline. Outside art, "memento mori" has become a popular motto in Stoic philosophy circles, printed on coins, tattoos and phone wallpapers. The meaning there is closer to the original: remembering death is a way to take life seriously.

FAQ

What is the difference between vanitas and memento mori?

Memento mori is the general reminder that everyone dies and can appear in any kind of art. Vanitas is a specific type of still life, mainly Dutch and Flemish, that pairs symbols of death with symbols of wealth, knowledge and pleasure to show that worldly things are empty.

Is a skull always a vanitas symbol?

No. A skull is first of all a memento mori. It becomes part of a vanitas only when the picture sets it against the luxuries and achievements it is meant to cancel out.

What does "vanitas" mean?

It is Latin for "vanity" in the old sense of emptiness or futility. The term comes from Ecclesiastes 1:2, "vanity of vanities, all is vanity".

Who were the main vanitas painters?

Key names include Pieter Claesz, Harmen and Pieter Steenwijck, David Bailly and Jan Davidsz de Heem in the Netherlands, and Philippe de Champaigne in France.
