An old man in a long red robe and a tall turban stands in an upper room of an old Ottoman building, a reed flute in his hands and a small drum on his back. At his feet, five tortoises munch on lettuce leaves, completely indifferent to his lesson. It is one of the gentlest jokes in the history of painting, and also one of the most debated. The Tortoise Trainer, painted by Osman Hamdi Bey in 1906, is the most famous painting in Turkey. What it actually means has been argued about ever since.

The painting at a glance

  • Artist: Osman Hamdi Bey (1842-1910), painter, archaeologist and founder of the modern Ottoman museum system.
  • Date: 1906 (a second version followed in 1907).
  • Medium: oil on canvas.
  • Where to see it: the 1906 version is in the Pera Museum, Istanbul. The 1907 version is in the İş Bank Museum of Painting and Sculpture, also in Istanbul.
  • Original title: in Turkish it was first called Kaplumbağalar ve Adam, "The Tortoises and the Man". The name Kaplumbağa Terbiyecisi, "The Tortoise Trainer", came later and stuck.

Who was Osman Hamdi Bey?

Osman Hamdi was one of the most remarkable figures of the late Ottoman Empire. Sent to Paris to study law in his youth, he trained instead as a painter in the circle of the French academic artists. Back in Istanbul he became director of the Imperial Museum in 1881, led excavations such as the one at Sidon that uncovered the so-called Alexander Sarcophagus, drafted laws to stop antiquities being taken out of the country, and founded the Academy of Fine Arts. He painted in the time left over, often using himself and his family as models. Many viewers believe the tortoise trainer is a self-portrait of the artist in his sixties.

What is happening in the scene?

The setting is an upper room with arched windows and tiled walls, the kind of space found in early Ottoman buildings; it is often linked to the Green Mosque in Bursa, a city Osman Hamdi knew well. The man wears old-fashioned dress from before the nineteenth-century clothing reforms. He holds a ney, the reed flute of Sufi music, and has a small drum slung at his back, as if he means to teach the tortoises to move to music. The tortoises take no notice whatsoever.

Where did the odd idea come from? One suggested source is an engraving of a Japanese "tortoise charmer" published in 1869 in the French travel magazine Le Tour du Monde, which Osman Hamdi could easily have seen. Whatever the inspiration, he moved the scene into an Ottoman interior and turned it into something quieter and more personal.

The main interpretations

A satire of slow reform. The most common reading sees the painting as a comment on the Ottoman Empire's reforms in its last decades. The old man represents an establishment trying to modernise with outdated methods, and the tortoises are the slow, unresponsive institutions or society he is trying to move. On this reading, nobody is getting anywhere, and the joke is on everyone.

A self-portrait of the museum director. Another reading focuses on Osman Hamdi himself. For decades he had tried to persuade officials and the public to value archaeology, museums and art education. The patient old man with his flute and his indifferent pupils can be seen as an affectionate, self-mocking picture of that struggle.

A meditation on patience and age. A gentler interpretation drops the politics altogether. The painting is about time, slowness and the dignity of trying anyway. The light is soft, the colours are warm, and the man shows no anger. Painted four years before Osman Hamdi's death, it can also be read as an old man's reflection on what one person can change.

None of these readings is confirmed by the artist, and they are not mutually exclusive. Part of the painting's power is that it lets each generation see its own frustrations in the tortoises.

The second version

A year after the first painting, Osman Hamdi painted a second, slightly different version. Most noticeably it includes six tortoises rather than five, and the composition and colours differ in detail. That 1907 version now hangs in the İş Bank Museum of Painting and Sculpture in Istanbul.

From Paris to the Pera Museum

The 1906 painting was exhibited at the Paris Salon of that year. It later passed through private hands, and in 2004 it was sold at auction in Istanbul to the Suna and İnan Kıraç Foundation, reportedly for about 3.5 million US dollars, a sum that made headlines in Turkey. It became the centrepiece of the Pera Museum, which opened the following year, and today the room where it hangs is one of the most visited spots in the city's art scene.

Why it matters

The Tortoise Trainer sits at a crossroads. Its technique is French academic realism, but its subject, setting and humour are entirely Ottoman. It was painted at the moment when an empire was trying to reinvent itself, by a man who spent his life building the institutions of that reinvention. For Turkish viewers it has become a shorthand for bureaucracy, patience and wasted effort, and the image turns up everywhere from newspaper cartoons to coffee mugs.

FAQ

What is the meaning of The Tortoise Trainer?

The most common interpretation is a satire on the slow pace of reform in the late Ottoman Empire. Others read it as a self-portrait of Osman Hamdi's own patient struggle to build museums and art education, or as a reflection on age and patience.

Where is The Tortoise Trainer painting?

The 1906 version is in the Pera Museum in Istanbul. A second version from 1907 is in the İş Bank Museum of Painting and Sculpture, also in Istanbul.

How many tortoises are in The Tortoise Trainer?

Five in the 1906 version and six in the 1907 version.

Is the man in The Tortoise Trainer Osman Hamdi Bey?

He often used himself as a model, and the figure is widely believed to be a self-portrait, though this is not documented by the artist.
