Persephone is the only figure in Greek myth who belongs fully to two worlds. For part of the year she is the daughter of Demeter, the goddess of grain, bringing spring with her. For the rest she is queen of the dead at Hades' side. Artists have spent more than two thousand years trying to show both halves at once, and they have developed a compact visual language to do it: a pomegranate, a torch, a handful of flowers, an open door. Once you know these signs, you can read almost any image of her.

The myth in brief

The oldest full telling is the Homeric Hymn to Demeter, composed in the archaic period. Persephone is picking flowers in a meadow when the earth opens and Hades carries her off in his chariot. Demeter searches the world for her daughter, carrying torches, and in her grief stops the crops from growing. Facing a famine, Zeus sends Hermes to bring Persephone back. But before she leaves, Hades gives her a pomegranate seed, and anyone who eats the food of the dead must return to them. The compromise: Persephone spends part of every year below and the rest above. Her descent is winter, her return is spring.

Later versions change details. In the Hymn she eats a single seed. In Ovid's Metamorphoses, the Roman retelling that shaped most European painting, she has eaten seven. The Romans called her Proserpina, which is why many paintings and sculptures carry that name.

Core symbols and what they mean

  • Pomegranate: the fruit that binds her to the underworld, and therefore the mark of her double life. Because it is full of seeds, it also stands for fertility and renewal, so it carries death and rebirth at the same time.
  • Torch: Demeter's search through the dark, and later Persephone's own safe passage between the worlds. In ancient cult images she often holds one.
  • Flowers: the meadow where she was taken. The Homeric Hymn names the narcissus as the bait Hades used. Flowers mark her as the maiden of spring.
  • Wheat or grain: her link with Demeter and the harvest cycle.
  • Crown and sceptre: her role as queen of the underworld, a figure of real authority rather than only a victim.
  • Doorways, caves, stairs: the threshold between the worlds. Artists use them to show the moment of passing through.

Persephone in ancient art

Greek vase painters most often showed the abduction or the reunion with Demeter. Persephone was also central to the Eleusinian Mysteries, the secret rites at Eleusis near Athens that promised initiates a better fate after death. In this context her images were about hope rather than tragedy. One of the most striking survivals is the wall painting of the abduction in a royal tomb at Vergina in northern Greece, dating from the fourth century BC, where Hades grips the struggling goddess in his chariot. Placing that scene in a tomb tells you how the ancients read it: as a story about death that ends in return.

Bernini: the abduction in marble

The most famous image of the myth is probably Gian Lorenzo Bernini's sculpture The Rape of Proserpina (1621-22, Galleria Borghese, Rome). Bernini was in his early twenties. He shows Pluto, the Roman Hades, lifting Proserpina as she pushes against his face, a tear on her cheek. Visitors are always drawn to his hand pressing into her thigh, where the marble seems to give like flesh. The word "rape" here comes from the Latin raptus, meaning abduction or seizure, which is how the title is used in art history. Bernini's version is all about the violent moment of capture, with none of the later cycle of return.

The Victorians and the pomegranate

Nineteenth-century British painters turned Persephone into a figure of longing and melancholy. Dante Gabriel Rossetti painted his Proserpine several times. The version of 1874 in Tate Britain shows her in a dim corridor holding the bitten pomegranate, a narrow patch of daylight falling on the wall behind her. The model was Jane Morris, and many viewers have read the picture as a portrait of a woman caught between two lives. Rossetti also wrote a sonnet to accompany it and inscribed it on the painting.

Walter Crane chose a different moment in The Fate of Persephone (1877): the meadow, the scattered flowers, the chariot rising from a cleft in the earth. It reads almost like a frieze, the whole story spread from left to right.

Frederic Leighton painted the happy ending. In The Return of Persephone (c. 1891, Leeds Art Gallery), Hermes lifts the pale goddess out of the dark mouth of a cave towards her mother, who reaches up with open arms into the sunlight. The composition is a vertical climb from shadow into light, which is the whole meaning of the myth in a single movement.

How to read a Persephone image

Start by asking which moment the artist chose, because each carries a different meaning. The abduction is about violence and loss. The eating of the seed is about choice, fate or entrapment. The return is about renewal and the bond between mother and daughter. Then look at the light. Painters almost always use darkness for the underworld and daylight for Demeter's world, and the placement of Persephone between them tells you which way she is moving. Finally, check what she holds. A pomegranate points to her tie to Hades; flowers or wheat point to her identity as Kore, the maiden of spring.

Why she still matters

Persephone has become one of the most reimagined goddesses in modern culture, from feminist readings that see her as a woman who claims power in the underworld, to novels and musicals that retell the story from her point of view. The images make the same point the myth always made: loss and return are part of the same cycle, and the seed that traps her is also the promise that she will come back. FAQ

What does the pomegranate symbolize in Persephone art?

It is the fruit she ate in the underworld, which binds her to return there each year. It therefore symbolizes her double life, and, because of its many seeds, fertility and rebirth as well.

How many pomegranate seeds did Persephone eat?

It depends on the source. The Homeric Hymn to Demeter mentions one seed, while Ovid says seven. Later retellings often use six, matching six months below and six above.

Why is Persephone shown with a torch?

The torch recalls Demeter's search for her daughter and Persephone's role as a guide between the world of the living and the dead.

What is the most famous artwork of Persephone?

Bernini's marble Rape of Proserpina in the Galleria Borghese and Rossetti's painting Proserpine in Tate Britain are the best known.
